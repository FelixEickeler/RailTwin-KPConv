# Shrink the sphere before chasing new architectures

The highest-value changes for this fork are mostly about configuration and data, not architecture. The biggest outlier is the input geometry. With **in_radius 8.9 m and first_subsampling_dl 0.04 m, the ratio is about 220**, more than four times the "≤ 50" ceiling KPConv's author gives. He names these two parameters as the only ones that really matter on a new dataset. Next come the evaluation protocol, the imbalance handling and the fine-tuning schedule. Each has cheap, author-backed or well-replicated fixes:

- **Training sampler:** a class-balanced sampler (`use_potentials=False` on the training set only), which is the author's preferred imbalance remedy.
- **Loss:** CE + Lovász, the standard pairing in LiDAR segmentation.
- **Voting:** fixed, whole-cloud voting, worth about +1 mIoU for KPConv.
- **Fine-tuning:** end it with full-network training rather than prolonged freezing.

Recipe changes borrowed from PointNeXt, PTv3 and KPConvX (AdamW, cosine schedules, label smoothing) are cheap but have weak KPConv-specific evidence. The KPConvX architecture is the one well-measured architectural gain (about +4 mIoU on S3DIS), but porting it is costly. For synthetic-to-real transfer, the literature says scene completeness and scan simulation matter more than sensor noise. It also says mixing in up to 50% synthetic data is safe, and that pseudo-label self-training with cross-domain mixing gives the largest gains when unlabelled real data exists. No published study quantifies the benefit of MLS→ALS emulation, so the fork's ALS augmentation is a hypothesis to validate, not established practice. The prioritized adoption list at the end ranks these by benefit against cost.

## KPConv's author says two parameters dominate, and the fork breaks the rule for one

Hugues Thomas (HT) is consistent across years of GitHub answers. "When changing the dataset there are only two parameters that really count: `in_radius` and `first_subsampling_dl`." First set dl as low as useful, but no lower than the data's point spacing. Then set **in_radius ≈ 50 × dl** ([#90](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/90)). He later calls 50 an upper bound: "keep a ratio bellow 50 between the two values, to have a reasonable network size" ([#147](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/147)). The paper itself uses 50 × dl0, with dl0 = 6 cm outdoors ([KPConv paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08889)). The upstream MLS configs match: **NPM3D uses in_radius 3.0 m at dl 0.06 m, and Toronto3D 3.0 m at 0.08 m** ([train_NPM3D.py](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/blob/master/train_NPM3D.py); [train_Toronto3D.py](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/blob/master/train_Toronto3D.py)). The fork's 4 cm dl is defensible. HT advises 0.03–0.05 m when small objects such as bollards or road markings matter ([#156](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/156)), and rail wires and droppers are of that scale. The 8.9 m radius is the anomaly. On DALES, where a large sphere ran out of memory, HT moved to a smaller radius and noted that reducing the sphere radius "I often found it improved the performances" ([KPConv #135](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv/issues/135)). Mix3D's crop study points the same way: KPConv gains little beyond about a 2 m sphere on ScanNet ([Mix3D](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02210)).

A mechanical argument supports this, though it is my inference rather than a sourced result. With five levels and dl doubling at each, the deepest grid is about 0.64 m and its conv radius about 1.6 m. The effective receptive field is therefore far smaller than an 8.9 m sphere. Most of the extra sphere adds labelled points per sample, not usable context. It also makes each batch huge, and HT notes that memory is set by points per batch, not by `batch_num` ([#189](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/189)). A smaller sphere at the same dl buys more independent spheres per step, faster epochs and more decorrelated gradients. The counter-argument is legitimate: masts, embankments and the track context that separates rail from ground are large. HT's answer is cross-validation, not a fixed rule ([#180](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/180)). The concrete experiment is in_radius ∈ {2.5, 4, 6} m at dl 0.04 m, scored with a fixed voting protocol and per-class IoU on wires, rails and masts. Two constraints apply. First, train and test must use the same dl, because filters are scale-specific and broad scale augmentation hurt HT's results ([#86](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/86)). Second, keep the core kernel parameters at their defaults: K = 15, conv_radius 2.5, KP_extent about 1.0–1.2, deform_radius 5–6 ([#90](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/90)).

HT's other guidance largely confirms what the fork already does:

- **Batch size:** `batch_num = 6` sits inside his band. He says to keep it above 3–5 for stability and saw no gain above about 10–16 ([#118](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/118); [train_NPM3D.py](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/blob/master/train_NPM3D.py)). If calibration is unstable, the knob is the hard-coded `expected_N`, which should be on the order of the expected batch limit ([#118](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/118)).
- **Optimizer and schedule:** SGD with momentum 0.98, LR 1e-2 and ×0.1 every 150 epochs is the paper and code default. He treats `epoch_steps` and `lr_decays` as interchangeable, and warns that compressing them too much stops training before the minimum ([#48](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/48)).
- **Input features:** keep the constant-1 feature always. He calls height above ground a potential "game-changer" for outdoor data and warns that raw absolute z misleads on sloped terrain ([#90](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/90); [#72](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/72)). The fork's `above_ground` feature is exactly what he recommends.
- **Colour dropout at test time:** upstream applies colour dropout in every split, including test ([datasets/S3DIS.py](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/blob/master/datasets/S3DIS.py)). The fork's restriction to training is a sensible deviation; HT never addresses it.

### Rigid kernels are the author's default for custom data

HT repeatedly tells users to drop deformable KPConv. "I would advise keeping the rigid convolution. It is much more simple, faster, and the results are not always worse" ([#90](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/90)). He also says the PyTorch deformable path never received tuning effort ([KPConv #134](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv/issues/134)). The paper's numbers split by dataset type:

| Dataset | Rigid | Deformable | Better |
|---|---|---|---|
| Semantic3D | **74.6** | 73.1 | rigid |
| Paris-Lille-3D | 72.3 | **75.9** | deformable |
| S3DIS | 65.4 | **67.1** | deformable |

The paper attributes deformable's weakness on Semantic3D to low class diversity, "a majority of ground, building and vegetation points" ([KPConv paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08889)). Rail corridors look more like Semantic3D, but Paris-Lille-3D is MLS too, so the outcome is genuinely uncertain. HT's successor, KPConvX, "chose to avoid deformable kernels" ([#270](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/270)). An A/B run of rigid against the current deformable list costs only a config edit and is worth running. Rigid at least delivers speed and memory savings, which can be spent on more spheres or more votes.

### Imbalance: the sampler first, class weights second, and `segloss_balance` does nothing

HT's preferred remedy for rare classes is the class-balanced sphere sampler (`use_potentials=False` on the training set). It draws N/C spheres centred on points of each class. In his tests, "class_w had nearly no effect, and the first option was the one which help the most" ([#2](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/2)). He repeated this in October 2025 for a heavily imbalanced mobile-mapping road dataset ([#266](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/266)). Validation must stay potential-based ([#147](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/147)). Upstream marks `segloss_balance` as DEPRECATED, and the loss only reads `class_w` ([utils/config.py](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/blob/master/utils/config.py); [models/architectures.py](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/blob/master/models/architectures.py)). Changing that string therefore changes nothing. The fork currently passes `use_potentials=True` to the training set in all train scripts (`train_rtib3+.py`, `train_rtib3+_tl.py`, `train_railtwin.py`). Every `rtib3p*` dataset already contains the `random_item` path the flag switches to, and `RTIB3pSampler` then picks sphere centres per class. The independent evidence is modest: class-aware sampling gave KPConv **only +0.6 mIoU on RELLIS-3D** ([RELLIS-3D](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.12954)). That is a reason to measure per-class IoU rather than mIoU when evaluating it.

### Upstream bug fixes are already in the fork

The two fixes that matter for custom data both appear in this repo's code. **d19c575** fixes rigid KPConv crashing on CUDA because `-1` shadow neighbours were redirected only in the deformable path ([commit d19c575](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/commit/d19c575); [#267](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/267)); it is at `models/blocks.py:281`. **435d117** fixes centre-point jitter that was added in place to the KD-tree's points, unbounded ([commit 435d117](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/commit/435d117)); the copy-and-clip version is at `datasets/rtib3p_reworked.py:349–353` and `574–578`. Two smaller items remain worth checking. HT advises wrapping validation in `torch.no_grad` to save memory ([#25](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/25)). He also advises cleaning isolated outlier points, which otherwise trigger "empty input spheres" errors ([#169](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/169)).

## Post-2019 recipes add perhaps 1–3 mIoU, and only KPConvX measured it on KPConv

KPConvX (CVPR 2024, by HT and co-authors) is the only authoritative "modernised KPConv". It raised **S3DIS Area 5 from 67.1 to 73.5 mIoU** ([KPConvX paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.13194)). Its ablation separates two sources. A new training recipe alone took the authors' re-implementation of plain KPConv to **68.5**. That recipe combines AdamW (wd 0.01, LR 5e-3, ×0.1 every 60 epochs), an effective batch of 24 through 6× gradient accumulation, Pointcept augmentations, a z feature and whole-room voting. The comparison is uncontrolled, because implementation details also changed. Architecture added the rest:

- **Depthwise (KPConvD) weights:** cut memory from 12.7 to 7.1 GB at a slight accuracy cost.
- **Deeper encoder** ([3,3,9,12,3] blocks) plus one decoder block per level: **+1.9**.
- **Inverted-bottleneck blocks:** **+1.3**.
- **Nearest-kernel influence:** 8.1 → 5.2 GB at no accuracy cost.
- **Kernel attention:** only **+0.2** over KPConvD-L, at roughly 1.7× the parameters.

The official code ([apple/ml-kpconvx](https://github.com/apple/ml-kpconvx)) is a standalone, Pointcept-format pipeline on PyTorch 2.3, not a patch for KPConv-PyTorch. Porting the depthwise operator and block design into the fork's `blocks.py` is the realistic route. KPConvX reports no outdoor or LiDAR results, so the +4 mIoU transfer to rail data is unproven.

PointNeXt gives the cleanest cumulative recipe ablation, though on a weak PointNet++ baseline ([PointNeXt](https://arxiv.org/html/2206.04670)). On S3DIS the steps were:

| Step | Gain (mIoU) |
|---|---|
| Colour drop | **+5.9** |
| Colour auto-contrast | +1.7 |
| Re-adding rotation once the model is scaled | +1.5 |
| Whole-scene input | +1.1 |
| Step → cosine LR | +0.7 |
| Adam → AdamW | +0.6 |
| Height append | +0.5 |
| Higher LR | +0.5 |
| Label smoothing (0.2) | +0.4 |

KPConv already has colour drop, rotation and a height feature. Rail MLS usually lacks reliable RGB, and synthetic IFC clouds have none, so the colour gains do not transfer. What remains to try is AdamW, cosine with warmup, and label smoothing, each worth a few tenths of a point on another backbone. HT reports AdamW ≈ SGD in his own tests, while one user reported a "significant mIoU boost" on a custom dataset ([#270](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/270)). Treat it as a cheap experiment, not a known gain. PTv3's outdoor recipe is AdamW with cosine, a 2-epoch warmup and CE + Lovász. Its outdoor augmentations are deliberately minimal: z-rotation, scale, flip and jitter ([PTv3](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.10035)). That suggests the fork's "pendulum" tilt neither helps nor hurts per published evidence. Upstream's outdoor defaults (x-symmetry, anisotropic scale 0.9–1.1, noise 0.001 m) are the reference to check against ([train_NPM3D.py](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/blob/master/train_NPM3D.py)).

On loss functions, **CE + Lovász-softmax** is the best-replicated choice in LiDAR segmentation. In SalsaNext's cumulative ablation it moved weighted-CE mIoU from **50.4 to 56.6** on SemanticKITTI, but on a range-image network ([SalsaNext](https://arxiv.org/html/2003.03653)). PTv3 uses it 1:1 for all datasets ([PTv3](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.10035)), and the OSDaR23 rail PTv3 baseline also uses it ([Münger et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.18213v1)). The counterweight is a July 2026 ICPR study of 11 imbalance methods. It found **uniform CE within 0.8–3.3 mIoU of specialised methods**, and that those methods "can substantially degrade performance if poorly tuned" ([arXiv 2607.21089](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21089)). Rail-specific evidence is mixed. Inverse-frequency weighting was neutral for PointNet++ on TLS catenary data but considerably hurt Point Transformer ([Ton et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9824632)). RailCloud-HdF used sqrt-inverse weights plus oversampling of rare-class crops ([RailCloud-HdF](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2024/123948/123948.pdf)). Lovász is the low-risk addition: it optimises the IoU surrogate directly and has no per-class weights to mis-tune. Expect gains concentrated in rare classes rather than a large mIoU jump. That expectation is an estimate; no KPConv ablation exists.

Voting is already in place, and it is worth controlling. In a like-for-like comparison, voting moved **KPConv from 66.3 to 67.2 mIoU** on S3DIS 6-fold, and other backbones by 1.5–3.2 ([Torch-Points3D](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.04642)). HT says 20 votes ≈ 100, but 1 vote is noticeably worse ([KPConv #49](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv/issues/49)). With the default `test_smooth = 0.95`, the vote average has an effective memory of about 20 passes ([utils/tester.py](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/blob/master/utils/tester.py)). The practical point is methodological. Every recipe change above is about the same size as the voting gain, so comparisons must use a fixed voting protocol on full clouds. HT calls the full-cloud voted mIoU "the real measure", not the per-epoch subset estimate ([#156](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/156)).

**Mix3D** is the one augmentation published *on KPConv itself*. Its authors mixed input spheres inside the official code's variable-size batches and kept hyperparameters unchanged. They report **+0.7 to +1.5 mIoU for rigid and deformable KPConv** across ScanNet, S3DIS and SemanticKITTI ([Mix3D](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02210)). PolarMix's rotate-paste of rare-class instances was its largest component: in the ablation it reached 53.2 from a 48.9 baseline, against 50.9 for simple paste ([PolarMix](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00223)). It needs no spinning-sensor structure, so it adapts to MLS if pasted objects are placed plausibly along the track.

## Rail benchmarks keep KPConv competitive, and thin catenary parts are the hard part

On railway MLS, KPConv remains a strong baseline:

- **Rail3D:** a fine-tuned KPConv reached **86% mIoU**, against 71% for LightGBM ([Rail3D / DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/acec61566f934ffbbddeed7e77c21c5f)).
- **KPConv pipeline on Belgian data:** trained on Rail3D, it reported 84% mIoU for rails, wires and poles ([ISPRS Archives 2024](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XLVIII-2-W8-2024/477/2024/)).
- **Newer backbones on SemanticRail3D:** **PTv3 82.95%** and Swin3D 82.47% ([Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/93262)).

These numbers are on different datasets with 8–14 classes, so they show parity in magnitude, not a ranking. No same-split KPConv-vs-PTv3 comparison was found. That weakens any case for switching backbones before the data and recipe issues are fixed.

Difficulty concentrates in fine components and rails. Aggregated "catenary wire" classes reach **90–96% IoU** on railborne MLS. Rail IoU there is only 52–76% ([RailCloud-HdF](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2024/123948/123948.pdf)). On TLS catenary arches, droppers peak at **0.54 IoU**, against 0.71 for contact wire and 0.89 for poles ([Ton et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9824632)). Intensity is the input feature with the clearest rail evidence. It lifted level-crossing-gate IoU from 35.2 to 49.5 for ConvPoint and catenary wire from 90.9 to 93.5 ([RailCloud-HdF](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2024/123948/123948.pdf)). The same paper shows the risk of density mismatch: ConvPoint fell from **74.4% to under 34% mIoU** when tested at grid sizes different from training. Cross-sensor tests on SemanticRail3D-trained PTv3, MinkUNet and Swin3D found "substantial performance variations across sensors" between TLS and handheld MLS ([ISPRS Annals 2026](https://isprs-annals.copernicus.org/articles/XI-2-2026/85/2026/)).

The open datasets offer a cheap lever. **SemanticRail3D** has 438 clouds and about 2.8 B points of MLS, under CC BY 4.0 ([Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/doi/10.5281/zenodo.11143767)). **Rail3D** is partly open with KPConv code. Both could serve as an intermediate real-data stage between synthetic pretraining and project fine-tuning. Neither separates droppers or cantilever parts, so a class-mapping layer would be required. The repo's own lineage, Eickeler & Borrmann's "priming" with CAD-derived synthetic survey data, reported **>50% shorter adaptation time** and better resilience for under-represented classes ([TUM portal](https://portal.fis.tum.de/de/publications/enhancing-railway-detection-by-priming-neural-networks-with-proje/)).

## Synthetic and ALS transfer hinge on scene fidelity, full fine-tuning and pseudo-labels

### Scene fidelity matters more than sensor noise

Where scene geometry is faithful, synthetic-only training nearly closes the gap. Where scenes are simplified, it does not:

- **Leaf-wood (HELIOS++ scans, KPConv):** **93.7% vs 94.7% OA** for synthetic- vs real-trained.
- **Urban (same setup):** **74.1% vs 82.4%** ([Esmorís et al. 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0924271624002569)). Simulated scanning also beat sampling points from mesh surfaces.
- **Urban CARLA clone of a real city (KPConv):** 100% synthetic reached **0.29 mIoU vs 0.45** real-only ([Mind the Domain Gap](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17959)). The errors came from geometric simplification and missing class-specific surface detail, not sensor noise. Synthetic data also *helped* surfaces that real MLS sees poorly (roofs 0.01 → 0.65 IoU).

For IFC-derived rail scenes, this points to:

- non-design clutter: vegetation, cable troughs, debris, ballast texture;
- as-built deviation from the design model;
- simulated scan trajectories and self-occlusion, rather than more Gaussian jitter.

SynLiDAR's PCT, which models sparsity and appearance explicitly, added **+2.2 to +3.5 mIoU** ([SynLiDAR/PCT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.05399)).

Intensity is where synthetic data and the fork's open feature slot collide. IFC-generated clouds have no calibrated intensity. Cross-city ALS work found that intensity helps within a dataset but **deteriorates generalisation across datasets** ([Xie et al. 2021](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ISPAr43B2..247X/abstract)). The defensible design adds intensity only at the real-data fine-tuning stage, normalised per sensor (as in the OSDaR23 PTv3 setup ([Münger et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.18213v1))). It should also be dropped per sphere with some probability, mirroring the existing colour dropout, so the geometry pathway learned during synthetic pretraining stays usable.

### Freezing costs more than it saves

The fine-tuning evidence argues against prolonged freezing:

- On S3DIS, training only the last layer gave **24.1% vs 50.0% mIoU** with full fine-tuning ([arXiv 2403.17869](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.17869)).
- A CVPR 2024 pretraining study fine-tuned the full network for outdoor segmentation ([CVPR 2024 supplement](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/supplemental/Zheng_Point_Cloud_Pre-training_CVPR_2024_supplemental.pdf)).
- SynthmanticLiDAR observed that the synthetic-pretraining advantage "seems to be lost after finetuning on real data" ([arXiv 2501.19035](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.19035)). This means synthetic data pays off mainly when real labels are scarce, which is exactly the railway project case.

Mixing is safe up to parity. KPConv at **50% synthetic / 50% real matched real-only (0.46 vs 0.45)** but dropped at 75% synthetic (0.39) ([Mind the Domain Gap](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17959)). No paper tests gradual unfreezing for LiDAR segmentation. The fork's head-first schedule is reasonable as a short warm-up. The early KPConv layers encode exactly the density statistics that differ between synthetic and MLS data, though, so the schedule should reach full-network training quickly. Keeping a synthetic share at or below 50% in later batches helps preserve rare classes that only synthetic data covers.

### Pseudo-label self-training gives the largest measured gains

If unlabelled real or ALS data is available, mixing source patches with pseudo-labelled target patches is the best-measured adaptation method. **CoSMix reaches 40.4 mIoU vs 20.7 source-only** on SynLiDAR→SemanticPOSS, and 32.2 vs 20.4 on SemanticKITTI. Plain Mix3D concatenation reaches only 28.5 in that setting ([CoSMix](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.09778)). On a KPConv backbone, LoGo's source-free self-training lifted **DALES (ALS) → Toronto-3D (MLS) from 56.1 to 73.5 mIoU** ([LoGo](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08375)). These are unsupervised-adaptation results. With labelled real data the margin over plain fine-tuning will be smaller.

### ALS emulation rests on a hypothesis, not a measurement

The fork's ALS emulation has precedent but no measured payoff. SegmentAnyTree states, as a hypothesis, that ALS can be approximated by down-sampling labelled proximal scans ([arXiv 2401.15739](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.15739)). No quantitative ablation of MLS→ALS emulation as augmentation was found, and no railway ALS↔MLS transfer paper. LoGo identifies three causes of the ALS–MLS gap:

- **Density:** about 50 vs 1000 pts/m².
- **Viewpoint:** nadir vs street-level, so facades or undersides are missing.
- **Batch statistics:** non-uniform MLS sampling skews them ([LoGo](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08375)).

Uniform thinning reproduces only the first. This repo's emulation already adds visibility from an airborne viewpoint (slope-tolerant z-buffer over flight strips, with returns), which also covers hidden points and the loss of vertical surfaces; HELIOS++ can simulate ALS directly from the same scene ([HELIOS++](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.09154)). Because HT's density guidance differs for ALS (dl about 0.25–0.3 m, in_radius 10–15 m ([#90](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv-PyTorch/issues/90); [KPConv #135](https://github.com/HuguesTHOMAS/KPConv/issues/135))), a model meant to run on *real* ALS likely needs its own scale configuration. Report emulated-ALS validation as a separate domain so its effect on MLS accuracy is visible.

## Conclusion

Most of the potential improvement in this fork lies in places that cost little to change. The fork already carries the upstream fixes and uses the features the author recommends. Its largest departure from published guidance is a sphere four times larger, relative to resolution, than the author's ceiling. Its largest unmeasured bets are ALS emulation and the gradual-unfreeze schedule, which no paper has validated. The literature's sharpest warning is that recipe gains (≤1–2 mIoU each) are the same size as the voting gain and as run-to-run noise. A fixed, voted, per-class and per-domain evaluation protocol is therefore the prerequisite that makes every other item measurable. The second insight is strategic. For synthetic-to-real transfer, investing in IFC scene completeness and simulated scanning, plus pseudo-label self-training on unlabelled real scans, has stronger evidence than any loss or optimizer change. The KPConvX architecture is the one large, proven architectural gain, but it belongs after the cheaper levers are exhausted.
